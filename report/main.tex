\begin{abstract}
The N-Queens problem is a classic combinatorial optimization benchmark requiring the placement of $N$ non-attacking queens on an $N \times N$ chessboard. This paper presents a comprehensive study of exact solving methodologies centered on Boolean Satisfiability (SAT). The primary contributions include the mathematical formulation, implementation, and empirical evaluation of five At-Most-One (AMO) encodings: Binomial (Pairwise), Sequential Counter, Binary, Commander, and Product. Furthermore, we conduct an extensive cross-paradigm benchmark against two other exact paradigms: Constraint Programming (Google OR-Tools CP-SAT, IBM CPLEX CP) and Integer Linear Programming (IBM CPLEX MIP, Gurobi). Computational experiments spanning baseline dimensions ($N=8$ to $30$), scalability extensions ($N=40, 50$), and massive stress-test instances ($N=100, 200$) reveal a severe trade-off between encoding size and solving latency. We quantitatively demonstrate that auxiliary variables—introduced to compress clause counts—can severely degrade Conflict-Driven Clause Learning (CDCL) heuristics. A cross-solver study across five modern backends (Glucose3, Glucose4, CaDiCaL, MapleSAT, and CryptoMiniSAT5) demonstrates that solver rankings reverse depending on the encoding, proving that solver--encoding synergy, rather than CNF size alone, dictates performance. Finally, a fairness evaluation under a first-feasible-solution stopping criterion confirms that the performance advantage of CP and MIP stems from high-level problem formulation and direct branching on original decision variables rather than termination discrepancies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Graph layout and resource allocation represent fundamental families of combinatorial optimization problems with pervasive applications in VLSI design, telecommunications architectures, and automated scheduling \cite{chung1988labelings}. Within this domain, the N-Queens problem serves as a classical and rigorous benchmark for evaluating the efficiency of combinatorial search and constraint reasoning algorithms.

The objective of the problem is to place $N$ queens on an $N \times N$ chessboard such that no two queens attack each other under standard chess rules (i.e., sharing neither row, column, nor diagonal). As the board size scales from small dimensions to large problem spaces, combinatorial explosion expands the state space factorially. Consequently, traditional depth-first backtracking algorithms rapidly succumb to computational exhaustion.

This study presents a complete and rigorous SAT-based methodology for solving the N-Queens problem exactly. By examining the interplay between Conflict-Driven Clause Learning (CDCL) heuristics and auxiliary boolean variables, we quantitatively analyze the performance paradox across diverse Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF) encodings. The remainder of this report is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews related work. Section 3 formalizes the mathematical problem formulation. Section 4 establishes five SAT encodings for At-Most-One (AMO) constraints along with symmetry breaking. Section 5 presents the Constraint Programming (CP) formulation. Section 6 provides comprehensive experimental benchmarks, scalability evaluations, cross-solver comparisons, fairness analyses, and runtime bottleneck discussions. Section 7 concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}
Transforming cardinality constraints into Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF) is a foundational and actively developed area in propositional satisfiability. Warners \cite{warners1998nonlinear} proposed linear-time transformations for linear inequalities using auxiliary variables. Sinz \cite{sinz2005towards} subsequently introduced the Sequential Counter encoding, achieving linear $O(n)$ clause complexity and establishing a standard building block in modern SAT applications. Gent and Nightingale \cite{gent2004generic} analyzed the trade-off between encoding compactness and unit propagation strength. For the specific At-Most-One (AMO) constraint, Klieber and Kwon \cite{klieber2007efficient} proposed the Commander encoding, while Chen \cite{chen2010new} designed the Product encoding based on 2D coordinate decomposition.

In terms of solver architectures, Conflict-Driven Clause Learning (CDCL) equipped with Variable State Independent Decaying Sum (VSIDS) decision heuristics \cite{marques2008conflict} constitutes the core engine of Glucose \cite{audemard2009predicting}, the primary SAT solver evaluated in this study. In parallel, Constraint Programming (CP) \cite{rossi2006handbook} preserves high-level integer constraint structures, filtering infeasible domains via maximum bipartite matching on \texttt{AllDifferent} constraints \cite{regin1994filtering}. Furthermore, Integer Linear Programming (ILP) / Mixed Integer Programming (MIP) \cite{bertsimas1997introduction} formulates combinatorial constraints as systems of linear inequalities solved via branch-and-cut; by branching directly on primary decision variables without auxiliary boolean variables, MIP avoids heuristic disorientation. Our study evaluates all three paradigms (SAT, CP, ILP) side by side on a uniform benchmark, illuminating how encoding structure and variable introduction impact solver performance.

\section{Problem Formulation}
The N-Queens problem can be formally modeled as a Constraint Satisfaction Problem (CSP). Given an $N \times N$ chessboard, let $x_{i,j} \in \{0, 1\}$ denote binary decision variables indicating whether a queen is placed at row $i$ and column $j$, where $i, j \in \{1, \dots, N\}$. The constraint system is defined by:
\begin{align}
    \sum_{j=1}^{N} x_{i,j} = 1 \quad \forall i \in \{1, \dots, N\} \label{eq:row} \\
    \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_{i,j} \leq 1 \quad \forall j \in \{1, \dots, N\} \label{eq:col} \\
    \sum_{i-j=k} x_{i,j} \leq 1 \quad \forall k \in \{-(N-1), \dots, N-1\} \label{eq:diag1} \\
    \sum_{i+j=k} x_{i,j} \leq 1 \quad \forall k \in \{2, \dots, 2N\} \label{eq:diag2}
\end{align}
Equation \eqref{eq:row} enforces the Exactly-One constraint for every row. Equations \eqref{eq:col}, \eqref{eq:diag1}, and \eqref{eq:diag2} enforce At-Most-One (AMO) constraints across columns and both sets of diagonals. Because an Exactly-One constraint naturally decomposes into an At-Least-One (ALO) clause and an AMO constraint, the core mathematical challenge lies in optimizing the CNF representation of AMO constraints.

\section{SAT Encodings for At-Most-One Constraints}
To leverage modern CDCL SAT solvers, the constraint system must be transformed into Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF). Given a set of boolean variables $X = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\}$, the AMO constraint enforces $\sum_{i=1}^n x_i \leq 1$. Below is the mathematical formulation of the five evaluated encodings.

\subsection{Binomial (Pairwise) Encoding}
The most intuitive formulation enforces mutual exclusion across all pairs of variables. For every pair $i \neq j$, both variables cannot simultaneously evaluate to True:
\begin{equation}
    AMO_{\text{Binomial}}(X) = \bigwedge_{1 \leq i < j \leq n} (\neg x_i \vee \neg x_j)
\end{equation}
This direct encoding introduces zero auxiliary variables. However, it incurs quadratic space complexity with $\binom{n}{2} = O(n^2)$ binary clauses.

\subsection{Sequential Counter Encoding}
To reduce clause volume, Sinz \cite{sinz2005towards} introduced $n-1$ auxiliary variables $s_1, \dots, s_{n-1}$ acting as an accumulator register. Variable $s_i$ evaluates to True if at least one $x_k$ ($k \leq i$) is True:
\begin{align}
    (\neg x_1 \vee s_1) \label{eq:seq1} \\
    \bigwedge_{i=2}^{n-1} \left( (\neg x_i \vee s_i) \wedge (\neg s_{i-1} \vee s_i) \wedge (\neg x_i \vee \neg s_{i-1}) \right) \label{eq:seq2} \\
    (\neg x_n \vee \neg s_{n-1}) \label{eq:seq3}
\end{align}
This encoding achieves a linear clause count of $3n - 4 = O(n)$, at the cost of introducing $n-1 = O(n)$ auxiliary variables.

\subsection{Binary, Commander, and Product Encodings}
\textbf{Binary Encoding:} Encodes the index of the active variable (if any) in binary using $\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$ auxiliary variables, producing $O(n \log n)$ clauses.

\textbf{Commander Encoding:} Partitions variable set $X$ into disjoint subsets of size $\approx \sqrt{n}$ \cite{klieber2007efficient}. Each subset is governed by a commander variable, achieving a balanced trade-off with $O(n \sqrt{n})$ clauses and $O(\sqrt{n})$ auxiliary variables.

\textbf{Product Encoding:} Arranges the variables into a 2D matrix of dimensions $p \times q$ with $p \approx q \approx \sqrt{n}$ \cite{chen2010new}. Setting $x_k$ to True activates its corresponding row and column auxiliary variables, yielding $O(n)$ clauses with $O(\sqrt{n})$ auxiliary variables.

\subsection{Symmetry Breaking}
Solutions to the N-Queens problem are invariant under horizontal reflection: if a board configuration is valid, its reflection across the vertical axis is also valid. We eliminate half of the search space without loss of completeness by fixing the queen in the first row to the left half of the board:
\begin{equation}
    \neg x_{0,c} \quad \forall c \in \left\{\left\lceil N/2 \right\rceil, \dots, N-1\right\}
\end{equation}
These unit clauses are injected identically across all five SAT encodings, ensuring that all solvers navigate the exact same pruned search space and guaranteeing rigorous benchmarking fairness.

\section{Constraint Programming (CP) Formulation}
Unlike SAT solving, which decomposes problems into propositional clauses, Constraint Programming (CP) preserves high-level integer domain abstractions. Defining an array of integer variables $Q = [Q_0, \dots, Q_{N-1}]$, where $Q_i \in \{0, \dots, N-1\}$ denotes the column coordinate of the queen placed on row $i$, the entire problem is captured using three global constraints:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{AddAllDifferent($Q$)}
    \item \texttt{AddAllDifferent($Q_i - i \;\forall i$)}
    \item \texttt{AddAllDifferent($Q_i + i \;\forall i$)}
\end{itemize}
Internally, CP engines propagate \texttt{AddAllDifferent} constraints using maximum bipartite matching filtering algorithms \cite{regin1994filtering}. This achieves generalized arc consistency (GAC), pruning infeasible subtrees without materializing millions of propositional clauses.

\section{Computational Experiments and Bottleneck Analysis}
\subsection{Experimental Setup}
Experiments were conducted across baseline dimensions $N \in \{8, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30\}$, and subsequently extended to two large scales ($N=40, 50$) and two massive stress-test scales ($N=100, 200$) to evaluate scalability frontiers and pinpoint breakdown points. Three distinct families of exact solvers were evaluated: (i) SAT solving with 5 encodings utilizing the Glucose3 CDCL backend; (ii) Constraint Programming with CP-SAT (Google OR-Tools) and CPLEX CP Optimizer (IBM); and (iii) Integer Linear Programming with CPLEX MIP (IBM) and Gurobi. For instances $N \leq 30$, each configuration was executed across 3 repetitions and averaged; for extended benchmarks, single runs were used for timeouts while converging runs averaged 3 repetitions. To quantify measurement noise, standard deviations as well as minimum and maximum solve times are reported. High-precision monotonic timing via \texttt{time.perf\_counter()} was employed to eliminate discretization artifacts. All solvers were restricted to single-threaded execution to ensure fairness. Solutions across all paradigms—not merely SAT—were independently validated by a shared verification oracle. Model generation time (Python) and solving time (solver core) were measured independently. A cross-solver evaluation across five backends (Glucose3, Glucose4, CaDiCaL, MapleSAT, CryptoMiniSAT5) and a first-feasible-solution fairness benchmark for CP/MIP (Section \ref{sec:fairness}) were also conducted.

\subsection{Runtime Bottleneck Analysis}
Inspection of Figure \ref{fig:runtime} uncovers a severe performance paradox in SAT solving: a smaller clause footprint does not guarantee faster execution.

At $N=10$, solve times across all methods are negligible; however, divergence manifests sharply for $N \geq 25$. At $N=30$, \textbf{Product} exhibits the most severe degradation ($14.45$s), followed by \textbf{Binary} ($9.65$s). Conversely, \textbf{Binomial} completes in $0.029$s total, while \textbf{Commander} requires only $0.248$s—roughly $58$ times faster than Product. Notably, Binomial achieves the fastest pure \emph{solve} time ($0.0126$s), outperforming even Commander ($0.2485$s).

    \label{fig:runtime}

\textbf{Bottleneck Explanation:} Performance degradation is governed not merely by auxiliary variable volume, but primarily by the \emph{implication chain length} and its alignment with unit propagation.

On the positive end, \textbf{Commander} maintains superior performance ($0.248$s at $N=30$, $58\times$ faster than Product) despite generating $1,606$ auxiliary variables. This efficiency arises because commander constraints are partitioned into localized sub-groups of size $\sqrt{n}$, enabling unit propagation to immediately eliminate an entire sub-group as soon as a variable is assigned True.

On the negative end, \textbf{Product} suffers catastrophic slowdown ($14.45$s at $N=30$). Arranging variables in a 2D matrix forces each queen variable to bind simultaneously to row and column auxiliaries, yielding elongated and diffuse implication chains. Glucose3's VSIDS heuristic becomes disoriented, prioritizing branching decisions on auxiliary variables rather than primary queen variables, inflating the search tree. \textbf{Binary} closely follows ($9.65$s). Meanwhile, \textbf{Sequential} occupies an intermediate tier ($0.208$s), as its sequential accumulator supports unit propagation far better than the 2D grid of Product.

Finally, \textbf{Binomial}—devoid of auxiliary variables—achieves the fastest solve time among SAT encodings ($0.0126$s). This reaffirms that CNF volume is secondary; the true computational penalty stems from heuristic dilution caused by auxiliary variables.

\subsection{Fair SAT vs MIP Benchmark: First Feasible Solution}
\label{sec:fairness}
A fundamental methodological issue when comparing SAT with ILP/MIP solvers is the \emph{termination criterion}. Pure SAT solvers address a \emph{satisfiability} decision problem: they terminate immediately upon identifying the \textbf{first feasible solution} without regard to objective functions. Conversely, MIP solvers (e.g., Gurobi, CPLEX) inherently solve \emph{optimization} problems, typically executing branch-and-cut to establish optimality (driving the optimality gap to zero). To verify whether this difference affects observed runtimes, we implemented a dedicated \textbf{first-solution} mode for CP/MIP solvers: Gurobi sets \texttt{SolutionLimit=1}, CPLEX MIP sets \texttt{mip.limits.solutions=1}, and CP-SAT sets \texttt{stop\_after\_first\_solution=True}. Table \ref{tab:fairness} compares both modes on identical benchmark runs (averaged across 5 repetitions).

\textbf{Surprising finding: the first-solution mode causes virtually no difference ($\Delta \approx 1.0$ across all tested instances).} Two key methodological factors explain this phenomenon. First, N-Queens is fundamentally a \emph{feasibility} problem rather than an optimization problem: the MIP formulations constructed here \textbf{contain no objective function}. Consequently, MIP solvers terminate immediately upon finding the first feasible integer solution—imposing \texttt{SolutionLimit=1} yields no acceleration. (Independent verification: Gurobi reports \texttt{SolCount=1} and \texttt{ObjBound=-inf}, confirming that no optimality proof is performed.) Second, in CP-SAT, \texttt{stop\_after\_first\_solution} merely omits optimality bounds, but at these scales feasibility is reached almost instantaneously. \emph{Thus, any conjecture that MIP is disadvantaged due to optimality proofs is refuted for this problem.} This further solidifies our central thesis: the performance disparity between SAT and CP/MIP does \textbf{not} arise from stopping criteria, but from fundamental differences in \emph{modeling paradigm}—MIP branches directly on primary variables without auxiliary noise. Even under identical first-solution termination criteria, Gurobi outperforms the best SAT encoding (Binomial) by up to two orders of magnitude.

Finally, non-SAT solvers demonstrate overwhelming dominance, with \textbf{Gurobi-MIP emerging as the overall fastest solver} ($0.00063$s solve time at $N=30$, approximately $20\times$ faster than Binomial and $23,000\times$ faster than Product). \textbf{CPLEX-MIP} follows closely with $0.0113$s. In the CP domain, \textbf{CPLEX-CP} ($0.0494$s) and \textbf{CP-SAT} ($0.0776$s) also comfortably outperform auxiliary-variable SAT encodings, though falling slightly behind Binomial ($0.0126$s). It is worth observing that Python modeling overhead for commercial solvers is nontrivial (CPLEX-MIP: $0.017$s, Gurobi: $0.0075$s), again reinforcing the necessity of separating formulation latency from solve latency.

\subsection{Scalability Verification at $N=40$ and $N=50$}
To verify that these dynamics extend beyond small-to-moderate scales, we evaluated $N=40$ under a 90-second timeout. Results (Table \ref{tab:scalability}) reveal a clear inversion from $N=30$: \textbf{Binomial emerges as the fastest SAT encoding} ($0.048$s solve time, 3-run average)—surpassing Sequential and vastly outperforming Commander. This underscores that as problem dimensions scale, generating $103,540$ clauses becomes negligible compared to the heuristic benefits of an auxiliary-free variable space. In contrast, both \textbf{Binary and Product exceed the 90s feasibility threshold}.

Remarkably, \textbf{Gurobi-MIP} preserves its commanding lead with only $0.028$s solve time, followed by \textbf{CPLEX-CP} ($0.034$s) and \textbf{CP-SAT} ($0.134$s). An important tooling observation: \textbf{CPLEX-MIP Community Edition} reached its 1,000-variable ceiling at $N=40$ ($1,600$ variables) and could not execute. These findings provide empirical confirmation that SAT feasibility is dictated primarily by encoding topology (auxiliary variables and implication chains) rather than problem scale alone.

\section{Conclusion}
This study provides a rigorous quantitative and theoretical investigation of exact solving methodologies for the N-Queens problem. Contrary to common intuition, compressing CNF clause counts by introducing auxiliary variables frequently induces severe performance penalties in large search spaces by corrupting CDCL branching heuristics. Crucially, the severity of degradation depends on the structural topology of the encoding rather than the raw auxiliary variable count: Commander preserves commendable performance due to localized grouping that fosters rapid unit propagation, whereas Product degrades catastrophically due to elongated, cross-matrix implication chains. Furthermore, this study demonstrates that for tightly constrained combinatorial problems, Constraint Programming (CP) and Integer Linear Programming (ILP) deliver vastly superior modeling abstractions and solving speeds compared to Boolean reduction. Scalability evaluations at $N=40$ and $N=50$ solidify these conclusions: while Binomial readily converges at $N=50$ (despite instantiating $203,425$ clauses), inefficient auxiliary encodings (Binary and Product) break down, while CP and ILP engines resolve instances in milliseconds. Stress-testing at $N=100$ and $N=200$ precisely establishes the \emph{breakdown boundary}: at $N=100$, Binomial solves in $0.183$s across $1,646,850$ clauses, whereas \emph{every} auxiliary-variable encoding exceeds the $40$s limit; at $N=200$, Binomial itself collapses under the weight of generating $13,253,700$ clauses in $4.6$s. Additionally, first-feasible-solution benchmarking reveals that termination criteria do \emph{not} explain the SAT vs. MIP/CP gap ($\Delta \approx 1.0$ across all instances), proving that the dominance of CP and MIP is driven by direct variable branching rather than solver stopping mechanics.
