\begin{abstract}
The N-Queens problem is a classic constraint satisfaction benchmark requiring the placement of $N$ non-attacking queens on an $N \times N$ chessboard. We study it as a controlled testbed for SAT encodings of At-Most-One (AMO) constraints. Five AMO encodings---Binomial (Pairwise), Sequential Counter, Binary, Commander and Product---are formulated, implemented and verified: all five are shown mechanically to be correct and to enforce arc consistency through unit propagation, so they do not differ in propagation strength. They are benchmarked with Glucose3 for $N$ from $8$ to $200$ and compared with Constraint Programming (OR-Tools CP-SAT, IBM CPLEX CP Optimizer) and Integer Linear Programming (IBM CPLEX, Gurobi). The encoding with the largest CNF, Binomial, is the fastest SAT encoding on the original order up to $N=100$ and never times out on any of the ten orders tested at $N = 30, 50, 100$, so CNF size does not predict solving time. A permutation study over ten equivalent variable and clause orders per configuration shows, however, that single-order measurements greatly exaggerate the differences between encodings: at $N=30$ the slowdowns of Product, Binary and Commander shrink by factors of $1083$, $515$ and $32$, respectively. Genuine differences emerge only at larger sizes, where Sequential, Binary and Product fail on most orders at $N=100$ while Commander remains as fast as Binomial. A five-backend comparison (Glucose3/4, CaDiCaL, MapleSAT, CryptoMiniSAT5) shows that the best backend depends on the encoding. A first-feasible-solution experiment confirms that stopping criteria do not explain the MIP results: Gurobi finds a solution at the root node ($N=30$) or after a single node ($N=40$). Finally, a noise analysis and a list of threats to validity delimit which orderings the data support.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The N-Queens problem is a classical benchmark for combinatorial search and constraint reasoning \cite{bell2009survey}. The objective is to place $N$ queens on an $N \times N$ chessboard such that no two queens attack each other under standard chess rules (i.e., sharing neither row, column, nor diagonal). The number of candidate placements grows factorially with $N$, so naive depth-first backtracking quickly becomes impractical.

It is important to state the scope of this study precisely. Finding \emph{one} solution of N-Queens is not computationally hard: explicit constructions place $N$ non-attacking queens in $O(N)$ time for every $N \geq 4$ \cite{bell2009survey}. The problem is used here not because it requires SAT, but because its constraint structure---a set of overlapping At-Most-One (AMO) constraints---is simple, fully understood, and scalable, which makes it a controlled testbed for comparing CNF encodings and solver paradigms.

This study presents a SAT-based methodology for solving the N-Queens problem exactly and examines how the choice of AMO encoding affects Conflict-Driven Clause Learning (CDCL) solvers. Beyond reporting runtimes, we check two methodological pitfalls that are easy to overlook: (i) whether differences in propagation strength actually exist between the encodings, which we verify mechanically, and (ii) whether runtime differences survive a change of variable and clause order, to which CDCL solvers are known to be sensitive. The remainder of this report is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews related work. Section 3 formalizes the problem. Section 4 establishes five SAT encodings for AMO constraints along with symmetry breaking. Section 5 presents the Constraint Programming (CP) formulation. Section 6 provides experimental benchmarks, an ordering-sensitivity study, scalability evaluations, cross-solver comparisons, fairness analyses, and threats to validity. Section 7 concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}
Transforming cardinality constraints into Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF) is a foundational and actively developed area in propositional satisfiability. Warners \cite{warners1998nonlinear} proposed linear-time transformations for linear inequalities using auxiliary variables. Sinz \cite{sinz2005towards} subsequently introduced the Sequential Counter encoding, achieving linear $O(n)$ clause complexity and establishing a standard building block in modern SAT applications. Gent \cite{gent2002arc} showed how a CNF translation can let unit propagation enforce arc consistency, and Bailleux and Boufkhad \cite{bailleux2003efficient} applied this criterion to cardinality constraints, making propagation strength a standard yardstick alongside encoding size. For the specific At-Most-One (AMO) constraint, Klieber and Kwon \cite{klieber2007efficient} proposed the Commander encoding, while Chen \cite{chen2010new} designed the Product encoding based on 2D coordinate decomposition.

In terms of solver architectures, Conflict-Driven Clause Learning (CDCL) equipped with Variable State Independent Decaying Sum (VSIDS) decision heuristics \cite{marques2008conflict} constitutes the core engine of Glucose \cite{audemard2009predicting}, the primary SAT solver evaluated in this study. CDCL solvers are also known to be sensitive to seemingly irrelevant properties of the input, such as the order of variables and clauses, so single-run comparisons can be misleading \cite{marques2008conflict}. In parallel, Constraint Programming (CP) \cite{rossi2006handbook} preserves high-level integer constraint structures; the \texttt{AllDifferent} constraint admits a generalized-arc-consistent filtering algorithm based on bipartite matching \cite{regin1994filtering}, although practical solvers choose their propagation level per constraint. Integer Linear Programming (ILP) / Mixed Integer Programming (MIP) \cite{bertsimas1997introduction} formulates the constraints as linear inequalities solved via presolve, primal heuristics and branch-and-cut. Our study evaluates all three paradigms (SAT, CP, ILP) side by side on a uniform benchmark.

\section{Problem Formulation}
The N-Queens problem can be formally modeled as a Constraint Satisfaction Problem (CSP). Given an $N \times N$ chessboard, let $x_{i,j} \in \{0, 1\}$ denote binary decision variables indicating whether a queen is placed at row $i$ and column $j$, where $i, j \in \{1, \dots, N\}$. The constraint system is defined by:
\begin{align}
    \sum_{j=1}^{N} x_{i,j} = 1 \quad \forall i \in \{1, \dots, N\} \label{eq:row} \\
    \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_{i,j} \leq 1 \quad \forall j \in \{1, \dots, N\} \label{eq:col} \\
    \sum_{i-j=k} x_{i,j} \leq 1 \quad \forall k \in \{-(N-1), \dots, N-1\} \label{eq:diag1} \\
    \sum_{i+j=k} x_{i,j} \leq 1 \quad \forall k \in \{2, \dots, 2N\} \label{eq:diag2}
\end{align}
Equation \eqref{eq:row} enforces the Exactly-One constraint for every row. Equations \eqref{eq:col}, \eqref{eq:diag1}, and \eqref{eq:diag2} enforce At-Most-One (AMO) constraints across columns and both sets of diagonals. Because an Exactly-One constraint naturally decomposes into an At-Least-One (ALO) clause and an AMO constraint, the core mathematical challenge lies in optimizing the CNF representation of AMO constraints. Columns need only AMO: $N$ queens in $N$ distinct columns already fill every column. (The MIP models state the column constraints as equalities, which is equivalent.) In the SAT encodings the cell $(i,j)$ is the propositional variable with index $(i-1)N + j$, and every AMO is applied only to diagonals with at least two cells.

\section{SAT Encodings for At-Most-One Constraints}
To leverage modern CDCL SAT solvers, the constraint system must be transformed into Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF). Given a set of boolean variables $X = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\}$, the AMO constraint enforces $\sum_{i=1}^n x_i \leq 1$. Below is the mathematical formulation of the five evaluated encodings.

\subsection{Binomial (Pairwise) Encoding}
The most intuitive formulation enforces mutual exclusion across all pairs of variables. For every pair $i \neq j$, both variables cannot simultaneously evaluate to True:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{AMO}_{\text{Binomial}}(X) = \bigwedge_{1 \leq i < j \leq n} (\neg x_i \vee \neg x_j)
\end{equation}
This direct encoding introduces zero auxiliary variables. However, it incurs quadratic space complexity with $\binom{n}{2} = O(n^2)$ binary clauses.

\subsection{Sequential Counter Encoding}
To reduce clause volume, Sinz \cite{sinz2005towards} introduced $n-1$ auxiliary variables $s_1, \dots, s_{n-1}$ acting as an accumulator register. The clauses force $s_i$ to be True whenever some $x_k$ with $k \leq i$ is True (the converse is not enforced), and forbid $x_i$ when $s_{i-1}$ is already True:
\begin{align}
    (\neg x_1 \vee s_1) \label{eq:seq1} \\
    \bigwedge_{i=2}^{n-1} \left( (\neg x_i \vee s_i) \wedge (\neg s_{i-1} \vee s_i) \wedge (\neg x_i \vee \neg s_{i-1}) \right) \label{eq:seq2} \\
    (\neg x_n \vee \neg s_{n-1}) \label{eq:seq3}
\end{align}
This encoding achieves a linear clause count of $3n - 4 = O(n)$, at the cost of introducing $n-1 = O(n)$ auxiliary variables.

\subsection{Binary, Commander, and Product Encodings}
\textbf{Binary Encoding:} Let $m = \lceil \log_2 n \rceil$ and introduce auxiliaries $b_1, \dots, b_m$. Writing $\beta_t(k) \in \{0,1\}$ for the $t$-th bit (most significant first) of the $m$-bit binary representation of $k-1$, each $x_k$ forces the bit pattern of its own index:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{AMO}_{\text{Binary}}(X) = \bigwedge_{k=1}^{n} \bigwedge_{t=1}^{m} \left(\neg x_k \vee b_t^{\beta_t(k)}\right), \qquad b^{1} = b,\; b^{0} = \neg b .
\end{equation}
Two true variables would demand two different bit patterns, which is impossible. This yields exactly $n\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$ binary clauses and $\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$ auxiliary variables.

\textbf{Commander Encoding:} Partitions $X$ into consecutive groups $G_1, \dots, G_g$ of size $s = \lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil$ (the last group may be smaller), so $g = \lceil n/s \rceil$ \cite{klieber2007efficient}. Each group has a commander $c_h$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{AMO}_{\text{Cmd}}(X) = \mathrm{AMO}_{\text{Binomial}}(c_1, \dots, c_g) \wedge \bigwedge_{h=1}^{g} \Big( \big(\neg c_h \vee \textstyle\bigvee_{x \in G_h} x\big) \wedge \bigwedge_{x \in G_h} (\neg x \vee c_h) \wedge \mathrm{AMO}_{\text{Binomial}}(G_h) \Big),
\end{equation}
so that $c_h \Leftrightarrow \bigvee_{x \in G_h} x$. Our implementation is the one-level variant (Klieber and Kwon apply the scheme recursively); it uses $g = O(\sqrt{n})$ auxiliaries and $\binom{g}{2} + \sum_h \big(1 + |G_h| + \binom{|G_h|}{2}\big) = O(n\sqrt{n})$ clauses. For $n \leq 4$ it falls back to Binomial.

\textbf{Product Encoding:} Arranges the variables into a 2D matrix of dimensions $p \times q$ with $p = \lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil$, $q = \lceil n/p \rceil$ \cite{chen2010new}; variable $x_k$ occupies row $r(k) = \lfloor (k-1)/q \rfloor + 1$ and column $c(k) = ((k-1) \bmod q) + 1$. With row auxiliaries $R_1, \dots, R_p$ and column auxiliaries $C_1, \dots, C_q$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{AMO}_{\text{Product}}(X) = \mathrm{AMO}_{\text{Binomial}}(R) \wedge \mathrm{AMO}_{\text{Binomial}}(C) \wedge \bigwedge_{k=1}^{n} \left(\neg x_k \vee R_{r(k)}\right) \wedge \left(\neg x_k \vee C_{c(k)}\right).
\end{equation}
These forward implications are exactly the clauses of Chen's encoding; we encode the two inner AMO constraints pairwise instead of recursively, which yields $2n + \binom{p}{2} + \binom{q}{2} \approx 3n$ clauses and $p + q = O(\sqrt{n})$ auxiliary variables. Because only forward implications are used, an auxiliary may be True while no variable in its row or column is True. This does \emph{not} weaken propagation on the original variables: if $x_k$ is True, unit propagation sets $R_{r(k)}$ and $C_{c(k)}$, falsifies every other row and column auxiliary, and thereby falsifies every other $x_j$ (two variables sharing a row differ in their column). For $n \leq 4$ the encoding falls back to Binomial.

\begin{notebox}{Technical Note: PySAT Cardinality Encoding Semantics}
\textbf{Note on PySAT encoding labels.} In addition to the five handwritten implementations in \texttt{encoder.py}, our codebase includes \texttt{pysat\_encoder.py}, which interfaces directly with PySAT's cardinality algorithms (\texttt{pysat.card.CardEnc}). It should be noted that certain PySAT encoding identifiers do not map one-to-one to the algorithms named above: specifically, \texttt{CardEnc} types \texttt{cardnetwrk} (Cardinality Networks) and \texttt{mtotalizer} (Modulo Totalizer) are generalized sorting/counter networks with topological structures distinct from our handwritten Commander and Product encodings. Therefore, the PySAT module serves primarily as an independent verification baseline for logical correctness: the unit tests enumerate every solution for small $N$ under each handwritten and each PySAT encoding and check that the solution sets coincide with a brute-force enumeration. All quantitative comparisons and structural evaluations in this report originate strictly from the five native implementations in \texttt{encoder.py}.
\end{notebox}

\subsection{Symmetry Breaking}
The solution set of N-Queens is closed under reflection across the vertical axis of the board: if a placement is valid, its mirror image is also valid. We eliminate roughly half of the search space without loss of satisfiability by restricting the queen of the first row to the left half of the board (indices are 1-based as in Section 3), which adds $\lfloor N/2 \rfloor$ unit clauses:
\begin{equation}
    \neg x_{1,j} \quad \forall j \in \left\{\left\lceil N/2 \right\rceil + 1, \dots, N\right\}
\end{equation}
For odd $N$ the middle column remains allowed, which keeps the constraint sound. These unit clauses are injected identically across all five SAT encodings, so all SAT configurations search the same reduced space. The CP and MIP models (Section 5 and the MIP counterpart of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:row}--\eqref{eq:diag2}) do \emph{not} include this constraint; since it only removes solutions that are mirror images of others, its effect on time-to-first-solution is small, but the asymmetry is listed among the threats to validity (Section \ref{sec:threats}).

\section{Constraint Programming (CP) Formulation}
Unlike SAT solving, which decomposes problems into propositional clauses, Constraint Programming (CP) preserves high-level integer domain abstractions. Defining an array of integer variables $Q = [Q_0, \dots, Q_{N-1}]$, where $Q_i \in \{0, \dots, N-1\}$ denotes the column of the queen placed on row $i$ (0-based, matching the implementation; one queen per row is implicit in the model), the entire problem is captured using three global constraints:
\begin{itemize}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
    \setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
    \item $\mathrm{AllDifferent}(Q_0, \dots, Q_{N-1})$ \hfill (columns)
    \item $\mathrm{AllDifferent}(Q_0 - 0, \dots, Q_{N-1} - (N-1))$ \hfill (diagonals $i - j$ constant)
    \item $\mathrm{AllDifferent}(Q_0 + 0, \dots, Q_{N-1} + (N-1))$ \hfill (anti-diagonals $i + j$ constant)
\end{itemize}
The same model is passed to CP-SAT (\texttt{AddAllDifferent}) and to CPLEX CP Optimizer (\texttt{all\_diff}). It has $N$ integer variables and three global constraints instead of hundreds of thousands of clauses. \texttt{AllDifferent} admits generalized arc consistency (GAC) via bipartite matching \cite{regin1994filtering}, but the propagation level actually applied is a solver choice; we used the default settings of each solver apart from single-threading and did not force GAC. The two CP solvers also differ in architecture: CPLEX CP Optimizer is a classical CP engine, whereas CP-SAT is a lazy-clause-generation solver whose core is itself a CDCL SAT engine that creates Boolean literals for the integer domains on demand. CP-SAT therefore does use auxiliary Booleans internally; what distinguishes it from our SAT pipeline is that the encoding is produced lazily and combined with native propagators, presolve and its own search heuristics.

\section{Computational Experiments}
\subsection{Theoretical Summary of the Five Encodings}
Before turning to the empirical results, Table \ref{tab:theory} consolidates the structural profile of each encoding: its auxiliary-variable count, clause complexity, and the strength of \emph{unit-propagation} inference. The relevant notion is arc consistency enforced through unit propagation (ACUP): a translation enforces arc consistency if every value that is logically removed by the constraint is also removed by unit propagation alone, without search \cite{gent2002arc,bailleux2003efficient}. For a cardinality translation, ACUP means that the CNF prunes exactly as aggressively as the original constraint.

Table \ref{tab:theory} rules out one tempting explanation for runtime differences. All five encodings are correct AMO translations and all five achieve ACUP: with respect to the queen variables, unit propagation prunes exactly as much under Product or Binary as under Binomial. ACUP holds for every size $m$, by a short argument per encoding: in Binomial each $\neg x_j$ follows from a binary clause; in Sequential $x_k$ forces $s_k \Rightarrow s_{k+1} \Rightarrow \cdots$, which falsifies every later $x_j$ through $(\neg s_{j-1} \vee \neg x_j)$, and forces $\neg s_{k-1} \Rightarrow \neg s_{k-2} \Rightarrow \cdots$, which falsifies every earlier $x_j$ through $(\neg x_j \vee s_j)$; in Binary $x_k$ fixes all bits, so every other $x_j$ has a clause whose bit literal is now false; in Commander $x_k$ sets its own commander, which falsifies the other commanders and hence all variables in other groups, while the pairwise clauses handle its own group; Product was argued in Section 4.3. The mechanical check for $m = 2..16$ confirms these arguments empirically; it does not replace them. Both properties are checked by \texttt{src/verify\_propagation.py} and by the unit tests, which additionally enumerate all solutions for $N \leq 7$ under every encoding and compare them with brute force. The encodings differ only in size and in how tightly the auxiliaries are tied to the original variables: in Sequential, Binary and Product an auxiliary can be True with no queen placed, while in Commander it cannot. Neither property changes which assignments are pruned by propagation, so any runtime differences must come from search dynamics (decision heuristics, clause learning), which we examine empirically in Section \ref{sec:ordering}.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
Experiments were conducted across baseline dimensions $N \in \{8, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30\}$, and subsequently extended to two large scales ($N=40, 50$) and two stress-test scales ($N=100, 200$) to evaluate scalability frontiers and pinpoint breakdown points. Three distinct families of exact solvers were evaluated: (i) SAT solving with 5 encodings utilizing the Glucose3 CDCL backend; (ii) Constraint Programming with CP-SAT (Google OR-Tools) and CPLEX CP Optimizer (IBM); and (iii) Integer Linear Programming with CPLEX MIP (IBM) and Gurobi. For instances $N \leq 30$, each configuration was executed across 3 repetitions and averaged; for extended benchmarks, timed-out configurations were run once and converging ones three times (except Sequential at $N=50$, run once). Standard deviation, minimum and maximum of the solve time are recorded in the CSV files alongside the mean. Timing uses the monotonic high-resolution clock \texttt{time.perf\_counter()}. All solvers were restricted to a single thread (\texttt{num\_search\_workers=1} for CP-SAT, \texttt{threads=1} for CPLEX MIP, \texttt{Threads=1} for Gurobi, \texttt{Workers=1} for CPLEX CP; the PySAT backends are sequential). Solutions across all paradigms—not merely SAT—were independently validated by a shared verification oracle. Model generation time (Python) and solving time were measured separately. For SAT, \emph{Solve} is the time of the \texttt{solve()} call only, excluding loading the clauses into the solver; for CP-SAT, CPLEX MIP and Gurobi it is the wall-clock time of the solve call, which includes the solver's internal model translation and presolve; for CPLEX CP it additionally includes starting the external \texttt{cpoptimizer} process and transferring the model. A cross-solver evaluation across five backends (Glucose3, Glucose4, CaDiCaL, MapleSAT, CryptoMiniSAT5) and a first-feasible-solution fairness benchmark for CP/MIP (Section \ref{sec:fairness}) were also conducted. Because three repetitions provide only a coarse estimate of dispersion, Section \ref{sec:noise} additionally reports coefficient-of-variation diagnostics and a dedicated 10-repetition re-measurement. Since a CDCL solver is deterministic for a fixed input, repeating the same CNF measures timer noise only; Section \ref{sec:ordering} therefore re-runs every SAT encoding on randomly permuted but logically equivalent CNFs.

\textbf{Environment.} All experiments ran on a laptop with an Intel Core i7-13650HX CPU and 24\,GB RAM under Windows 11, using Python 3.12, PySAT 1.9 (Glucose 3.0 and the other backends bundled with it), Google OR-Tools 9.15, IBM CPLEX 22.2 Community Edition (\texttt{cplex} 22.2.0.1 and \texttt{docplex} 2.32 for MIP; CP Optimizer from CPLEX Optimization Studio Community 22.2), and Gurobi 13.0 with the size-limited pip licence. Each SAT solve runs in a separate process so that a timeout can be enforced; in the original runs the timeout clock started before the CNF was transferred to that process, which matters only for the multi-million-clause instances (see Section \ref{sec:threats}). The harness has since been corrected to start the clock after the CNF is loaded. The supplementary experiments (ordering study, CPLEX CP re-run, noise re-measurement) used the same machine and library versions; the $N=100$ part of the ordering study was run under Python 3.14 instead of 3.12, which does not affect the native solver code.

At $N=100$, Binomial generates \textbf{1,646,850 clauses}—over $38$ times more than at $N=30$—yet solves in merely \textbf{$0.183$s}. In contrast, \emph{all} auxiliary-variable encodings (Sequential, Commander, Binary, Product) \textbf{exceed the $40$s feasibility threshold}, despite producing only $\frac{1}{14}$ to $\frac{1}{6}$ as many clauses. At $N=200$, Binomial itself breaks down: it takes $4.6$s merely to \emph{generate} $13{,}253{,}700$ clauses and does not finish within the limit. In the original harness roughly half of the 40s budget was spent transferring and loading this CNF into the solver process; a re-run with the corrected harness, whose clock starts only after loading (\texttt{results/binomial\_n200\_long\_run.csv}), confirms, however, that Glucose3 does not finish within $300$s of pure solving time, so the conclusion stands. CP-SAT, which builds its Boolean representation lazily, solves $N=100$ in $0.64$s and $N=200$ in $5.9$s. Note that the time limits in this table differ between SAT (40s) and CP-SAT (60s); since CP-SAT finished far below either limit, this does not affect the comparison.

\subsection{Encoding-Size vs Runtime Analysis}
Experimental findings in Figure \ref{fig:clauses} corroborate theoretical predictions: although each Binomial AMO is quadratic in its length, the whole Binomial CNF grows cubically in $N$ because there are $\Theta(N)$ lines of length up to $N$, yielding 203,425 clauses at $N=50$ (compared to 43,255 at $N=30$). In contrast, Sequential, Binary, Commander, and Product maintain compact formulas ranging between 28,887 and 57,219 clauses at $N=50$, achieved through auxiliary variable compression. Consequently, the gap in formula size widens substantially with larger board sizes. For Binomial the total can be stated exactly. Rows contribute $N$ ALO clauses and $N\binom{N}{2}$ AMO clauses, columns another $N\binom{N}{2}$, each diagonal direction $\sum_{\ell} \binom{\ell}{2} = 2\binom{N}{3} + \binom{N}{2}$ over its diagonals of length $\ell$, and symmetry breaking $\lfloor N/2 \rfloor$ unit clauses, giving
\begin{equation}
    |\mathrm{CNF}_{\text{Binomial}}(N)| = \frac{5N^3 - 6N^2 + 4N}{3} + \left\lfloor \frac{N}{2} \right\rfloor,
\end{equation}
which reproduces the measured counts exactly ($43{,}255$ at $N=30$, $1{,}646{,}850$ at $N=100$, $13{,}253{,}700$ at $N=200$).

\subsection{Measurement Noise and Statistical Reliability}
\label{sec:noise}
The repeated measurements reveal a systematic pattern in their dispersion that must be addressed before the rankings below are interpreted. We use the coefficient of variation (CV, the population standard deviation of the three repetitions divided by their mean). For the SAT encodings the CV at $N=30$ is $0.4$--$4.8\%$ and stays below $5\%$ for all $N \geq 20$. The two CP solvers, whose runtimes are a few to a few tens of milliseconds, are much noisier at small sizes: across $N=8$--$30$, CP-SAT shows CVs of $3.9$--$44.4\%$ and CPLEX-CP of $3.9$--$43.0\%$.

We attribute this mainly to fixed overheads rather than to the search itself: when the measured interval is only milliseconds long, one-off costs such as initialisation and operating-system scheduling dominate. A dedicated re-measurement of CP-SAT with 10 repetitions per size (\texttt{results/cpsat\_noise\_per\_run.csv}, sample standard deviation) shows the CV decreasing as the measured interval lengthens: $147\%$ at $N=8$ (mean $5.3$ms), $11.4\%$ at $N=30$ ($38$ms) and $1.4\%$ at $N=50$ ($116$ms). At $N=8$ the dispersion is caused almost entirely by the first repetition ($27.7$ms versus a mean of $2.8$ms for the other nine), consistent with one-off initialisation; at $N=30$ and $N=50$ the first repetition is not slower than the rest. Once solving takes more than about a hundred milliseconds, the measurement stabilises.

The practical consequence is that differences between millisecond-scale configurations must be checked against the noise before they are interpreted. At $N=30$ CP-SAT averages $0.0776 \pm 0.0188$s and CPLEX-CP $0.0157 \pm 0.0006$s (mean $\pm$ one standard deviation); the gap of $0.062$s is $3.3$ times the combined standard deviation and even the slowest CPLEX-CP repetition ($0.0162$s) is faster than the fastest CP-SAT run at $N=30$ in any session ($0.0349$s), so CPLEX-CP is faster in wall-clock terms. With only three repetitions, however, no formal significance test is meaningful. Note also that CPLEX-CP's wall-clock time is almost entirely overhead: the engine itself reports about $2$ms of solve time at $N=8$, $30$ and $50$, the remainder being the start-up of the external \texttt{cpoptimizer} process and model transfer. Its nearly constant $15$--$19$ms across all $N$ reflects this.

Within-session dispersion also understates the true uncertainty. CP-SAT at $N=30$ averaged $0.078$s in the main benchmark, $0.038$s in the 10-repetition re-measurement and $0.036$s in the first-solution benchmark (Table \ref{tab:fairness}): the spread between sessions is larger than any within-session standard deviation. Millisecond-scale timings should therefore be read as order-of-magnitude indications only. For the SAT encodings an even larger source of variation exists that repetition cannot reveal at all, namely sensitivity to input order (Section \ref{sec:ordering}).

Four observations follow.

\textbf{(1) The original order is unfavourable for every encoding.} In $13$ of the $15$ configurations the original order is the slowest of the ten runs or times out; even Binomial is $5.8\times$, $41.7\times$ and $6.8\times$ slower on its original order than its median at $N = 30, 50, 100$. The row-major numbering of the queen variables, with auxiliaries appended at the end, is therefore not a neutral choice.

\textbf{(2) At $N=30$ most of the apparent gaps are artefacts of that order.} Product drops from $13.3$s to a median of $0.012$s ($1083\times$), Binary from $6.4$s to $0.012$s ($515\times$) and Commander from $0.24$s to $0.008$s ($32\times$). Their medians are at most $6.1\times$ Binomial's ($0.002$s), whereas the original-order results of Table \ref{tab:runtime} suggested gaps of up to three orders of magnitude.

\textbf{(3) At larger $N$ genuine differences remain.} Sequential is the only encoding that is already slow at $N=30$ on most orders (median $74\times$ Binomial's); at $N=50$ its median is $12.6$s with $3/10$ timeouts, and at $N=100$ all ten runs time out. Binary and Product survive most permutations at $N=50$ (medians $0.024$s and $1.36$s) but fail on $9/10$ and $8/10$ orders at $N=100$. Their solved runs at $N=100$ ($0.41$s and $0.04$--$0.28$s) show that the instance is easy for these encodings in principle, but the solver rarely reaches such a run.

\textbf{(4) Binomial and Commander are robust.} Both solve every permuted CNF at every size, with medians of $0.027$s and $0.025$s at $N=100$; the earlier conclusion that ``every auxiliary-variable encoding fails at $N=100$'' (Table \ref{tab:extralarge}) is true only for the original order and does not hold for Commander.

Note finally that Commander is the only auxiliary encoding whose auxiliaries are functionally defined (Table \ref{tab:theory}), while the three encodings that degrade at $N=100$ all have auxiliaries that can be True with no queen placed. This correlation is consistent with the hypothesis that unconstrained auxiliaries give the solver many irrelevant decision points, but with five encodings it is an observation, not a demonstration.

\begin{insightbox}{Interpreting the SAT Results}
\textbf{What the data support.} (i) CNF size does not predict solve time: Binomial, the largest encoding, never times out in the ordering study and has the lowest median time at $N=30$ and $50$; at $N=100$ only Commander matches it ($0.025$s vs.\ $0.027$s). (ii) Propagation strength does not explain the differences, since all five encodings are ACUP (Table \ref{tab:theory}). (iii) Single-order measurements exaggerate the gaps: at $N=30$ the slowdowns of Binary, Commander and Product shrink by factors of $515$, $32$ and $1083$ once the order is randomised (Table \ref{tab:ordering}). (iv) Genuine differences appear at larger $N$: Sequential degrades consistently from $N=30$ on, and Binary and Product fail on most orders at $N=100$, while Commander stays as fast as Binomial.

\smallskip
\textbf{What remains a hypothesis.} Two mechanisms are plausible but were not measured. First, auxiliaries that are not functionally defined (Sequential, Binary, Product) give the decision heuristic variables whose values carry no information about the board; Commander, whose auxiliaries are defined, is the only auxiliary encoding that stays robust. Second, Sequential's chain $s_1 \Rightarrow s_2 \Rightarrow \cdots \Rightarrow s_{n-1}$ makes conflicts span many decision levels, which may produce learned clauses with high Literal Block Distance (LBD) that Glucose deletes early \cite{audemard2009predicting}. Testing either explanation would require solver statistics (decisions on auxiliary variables, LBD distributions), which our harness does not record.
\end{insightbox}

\subsection{Fair SAT vs MIP Benchmark: First Feasible Solution}
\label{sec:fairness}
A fundamental methodological issue when comparing SAT with ILP/MIP solvers is the \emph{termination criterion}. Pure SAT solvers address a \emph{satisfiability} decision problem: they terminate immediately upon identifying the \textbf{first feasible solution} without regard to objective functions. Conversely, MIP solvers (e.g., Gurobi, CPLEX) inherently solve \emph{optimization} problems, typically executing branch-and-cut to establish optimality (driving the optimality gap to zero). To verify whether this difference affects observed runtimes, we implemented a dedicated \textbf{first-solution} mode for CP/MIP solvers: Gurobi sets \texttt{SolutionLimit=1}, CPLEX MIP sets \texttt{mip.limits.solutions=1}, and CP-SAT sets \texttt{stop\_after\_first\_solution=True}. Table \ref{tab:fairness} compares both modes on identical benchmark runs (averaged across 5 repetitions).

\textbf{Surprising finding: the first-solution mode causes virtually no difference ($\Delta \approx 1.0$ across all tested instances).} Two key methodological factors explain this phenomenon. First, N-Queens is fundamentally a \emph{feasibility} problem rather than an optimization problem: the MIP formulations constructed here \textbf{contain no objective function}. Consequently, MIP solvers terminate immediately upon finding the first feasible integer solution---imposing \texttt{SolutionLimit=1} yields no acceleration. (Independent verification: for $N=30$ Gurobi reports \texttt{SolCount=1}, \texttt{ObjVal=ObjBound=0} and \texttt{NodeCount=0}; at $N=40$ it explores a single node.) Second, CP-SAT has no objective either, so \texttt{stop\_after\_first\_solution} changes nothing. \emph{Thus, any conjecture that MIP is disadvantaged due to optimality proofs is refuted for this problem.}

The node counts also tell us \emph{why} Gurobi is fast: at $N=20$ and $30$ it does not branch at all, and at $N=40$ it needs a single node. The solution is found during presolve and root-node primal heuristics, so its advantage cannot be attributed to ``branching on primary variables''; it reflects the strength of these generic heuristics on a highly structured assignment-type model. Under the first-solution criterion Gurobi ($0.000335$s at $N=30$) is about $37\times$ faster than the fastest Glucose3 configuration (Binomial, $0.0126$s).

Overall, \textbf{Gurobi-MIP is the fastest solver at $N \leq 30$} in the default runs ($0.00063$s solve time at $N=30$, about $20\times$ faster than Binomial); at $N=40$ CPLEX-CP ($0.016$s) is faster than Gurobi ($0.028$s). \textbf{CPLEX-MIP} follows with $0.0113$s. In the CP domain, \textbf{CPLEX-CP} ($0.0157$s) and \textbf{CP-SAT} ($0.0776$s) both lie \emph{behind} Binomial with Glucose3 ($0.0126$s). The paradigm ranking is thus not ``CP/MIP above SAT'': a well-chosen SAT encoding is competitive with both CP solvers. It is worth observing that Python modeling overhead for commercial solvers is nontrivial (CPLEX-MIP: $0.017$s, Gurobi: $0.0075$s), again reinforcing the necessity of separating formulation latency from solve latency.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
\begin{itemize}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
    \setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
    \item \textbf{Single input order.} Tables \ref{tab:extralarge}, \ref{tab:runtime}, \ref{tab:crosssolver}, \ref{tab:scalability} and \ref{tab:scalability50} and Figure \ref{fig:runtime} use one fixed variable and clause order per instance. Section \ref{sec:ordering} shows that this order alone can change Glucose3 runtimes by two to three orders of magnitude, so per-cell comparisons in those tables should not be over-interpreted.
    \item \textbf{One instance per size.} N-Queens has a single satisfiable instance per $N$; results may not transfer to other problems built from AMO constraints.
    \item \textbf{Different time limits.} The limits differ between experiments (40, 60, 90 and 100s). Timed-out entries are reported as such and never averaged with finished runs.
    \item \textbf{Timeout accounting.} In the original runs the SAT timeout included transferring the CNF to the solver process. This is negligible below $10^6$ clauses but consumed about half of the budget for Binomial at $N=200$ (Table \ref{tab:extralarge}); the harness now starts the clock after loading.
    \item \textbf{Re-measured CPLEX-CP.} The original CPLEX-CP runs did not set \texttt{Workers=1}, so CP Optimizer used all CPU cores. All CPLEX-CP figures in this report come from a single-threaded re-run; the original multi-threaded numbers are kept in \texttt{results/cplexcp\_multithread\_original.csv}.
    \item \textbf{Asymmetric symmetry breaking.} Only the SAT encodings include the first-row symmetry-breaking clauses.
    \item \textbf{Licence limits.} CPLEX Community Edition and the pip Gurobi licence cap model size, so MIP results are missing at $N \geq 40$ (CPLEX) and $N \geq 50$ (Gurobi).
    \item \textbf{Timing noise.} Millisecond-scale timings vary between sessions by more than $2\times$ (Section \ref{sec:noise}). Even long deterministic runs vary: the identical original-order CNF of Binary at $N=30$ took $9.65$s in the main benchmark and $6.41$s in the ordering study, presumably owing to CPU frequency and thermal state of the laptop. This is small compared with the ordering effects but rules out precise cross-session ratios; in particular, absolute times in Table \ref{tab:ordering} and in Tables \ref{tab:runtime}--\ref{tab:scalability50} come from different sessions and should not be compared digit by digit.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
This study evaluated five SAT encodings of the AMO constraint on the N-Queens problem, together with CP and MIP solvers, and checked its own measurements for two common pitfalls. Its findings are as follows.

\begin{itemize}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
    \setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
    \item \textbf{All five encodings are correct and ACUP.} Differences in solving time therefore cannot be attributed to weaker unit propagation; they arise from search dynamics.
    \item \textbf{CNF size does not predict speed.} Binomial produces the largest CNF ($\frac{5N^3-6N^2+4N}{3} + \lfloor N/2 \rfloor$ clauses) yet is the fastest SAT encoding on the original order up to $N=100$ and never times out on any of the 10 orders tested. Its limit is size itself: at $N=200$ the $13.25$ million clauses take $4.6$s to generate and Glucose3 does not finish within $300$s of solving.
    \item \textbf{Single-order results are unreliable.} The original variable order was among the slowest of ten equivalent orders in $13$ of $15$ configurations. At $N=30$ the apparent gaps between encodings shrink from up to three orders of magnitude to at most $6.1\times$ Binomial's median once the order is randomised (Sequential excepted).
    \item \textbf{Genuine differences appear at scale.} Sequential degrades consistently from $N=30$ on and fails on every order at $N=100$; Binary and Product fail on $9/10$ and $8/10$ orders at $N=100$; Commander remains as fast as Binomial ($0.025$s vs.\ $0.027$s median). The three degrading encodings are exactly those whose auxiliaries are not functionally defined, a correlation that merits further study with solver statistics.
    \item \textbf{Paradigms.} Gurobi solves every size its licence admits (up to $N=40$) in at most $0.03$s, with zero branch-and-bound nodes at $N=20$ and $30$ and one at $N=40$; CPLEX CP needs $15$--$19$ms, almost all of it process overhead; CP-SAT scales to $N=200$ in $5.9$s. A good (encoding, solver) pair is competitive: CryptoMiniSAT5 with Binomial solves $N=30$ in $1.05$ms, faster than both CP solvers and CPLEX MIP. Stopping criteria do not explain the differences, since the MIP models have no objective.
    \item \textbf{Methodology.} Repeating a deterministic solver on one input measures only timer noise; between-session variation exceeds within-session variation for millisecond-scale timings; and the CPLEX CP configuration had to be re-measured after a threading error was found. Future work should report distributions over input permutations and record solver statistics to test the mechanisms proposed in Section \ref{sec:ordering}.
\end{itemize}
